Why focus fades over an hour

The first twenty minutes of a long, quiet task feel sharp. By the end of the hour you are rereading the same line and missing things you would have caught at the start. That slide has a name, it has been measured for nearly eighty years, and nobody yet agrees on why it happens. Tonight we look at the drop itself, the three explanations still fighting over it, the evidence on whether a walk among trees puts attention back, and then we plan an afternoon that expects the drift instead of being surprised by it.

Part 1 · covers the vigilance decrement

The decrement is real

5 min live

Norman Mackworth's clock test of 1948 is the classic starting point: a watcher follows a moving clock hand for a long stretch and has to spot its rare irregular jumps. This is reported here as the classic origin; the 1948 report was not read, so no number from it is quoted. The finding that followed, across decades of tasks, is what researchers call the vigilance decrement: when you watch for rare events, detection falls with time on task.

LiveWatching is work, not idling3 min▶

For a long time vigilance was treated as undemanding. The task looks passive: you sit, you watch, almost nothing happens. So the decrement was explained as under-arousal, the brain drifting toward sleep for lack of stimulation.

In 2008 Joel Warm, Raja Parasuraman and Gerald Matthews reviewed the evidence from several directions at once: how performance changes with the type of task and the workload, measures of blood flow in the brain recorded with transcranial Doppler, and reports of stress from the people doing the watching. Their conclusion is in the title: vigilance requires hard mental work and is stressful. That fits a resource account, where holding attention on a rare-event task draws on a limited capacity, better than a boredom account. The review is read here at the level of its abstract, so no number from its body is quoted.

For Tomas the practical point is simple. Checking a migration row by row feels like nothing is happening, and that is exactly why it tires him. The quiet task is not the easy one.

Self-studyWhat "robust" means here, and what it does not2 min▶

The decrement shows up across many tasks and decades of research, which is why the reviews take it as given and argue about the cause instead. Robust means the shape repeats: detection of rare targets falls with time on task. It does not mean there is one universal curve with a known slope, a fixed number of minutes before it starts, or the same drop for every person and every job.

That is why the figure above carries no numbers on its vertical axis. Session 4 puts a modelled drift on a go/no-go task, with its settings stated, so you can see how block length changes the slope in that model. Your own drift is something you estimate from your own notes, not something you read off a chart.

Part 2 · covers why it fades: the contested part

Three explanations, one fight

6 min live

Everyone agrees that performance falls. They disagree on what is falling. Three accounts compete, and each one predicts something different about what a break should do. That is why the fight is worth five minutes of a working person's time: your break habits quietly assume one of them.

LiveDepletion, mindlessness, habituation3 min▶

Resource depletion says sustained attention spends a limited capacity, and performance falls as it runs low. Warm and colleagues' 2008 review leans this way. If it is right, a break works by resting.

Mindlessness, or mind-wandering, says the opposite of hard work: a monotonous task fails to hold the mind, attention turns inward to your own thoughts, and the misses follow. Session 2's material on mind-wandering is the same process seen from the inside. If this is right, a break works by interrupting the drift.

Goal habituation is the newest of the three. Atsunori Ariga and Alejandro Lleras argued in 2011 that the mind habituates to a goal held without change, the way you stop hearing a fan. In their study, people who briefly switched to a different goal during the task, recalling digits they had memorised beforehand, did not show the decrement. The authors call that a direct challenge to depletion: an extra task is more work, not less, yet it helped. Their study is read here at the level of its abstract.

Self-studyThe resource-control account, and why nothing is settled3 min▶

In 2015 David Thomson, Derek Besner and Daniel Smilek reviewed the depletion and mind-wandering literatures and concluded that neither theory on its own explains the findings. They proposed a resource-control account that combines both: the resources for the task may not run out as such, but the control that keeps them pointed at the task weakens over time, and attention slides toward your own thoughts.

So the honest summary is three accounts and one proposed merger, none of them winning outright. You do not need to pick a side to act. A break that combines a little rest, a change of scene and a deliberate reminder of the goal is consistent with all three, which is the design the build-along uses.

Self-studyEgo depletion is a different claim, and it failed3 min▶

"Ego depletion" is the idea that self-control draws on a resource that runs out: resist the biscuits, and you will give up sooner on the next hard puzzle. It sounds like a cousin of the depletion account above, and it is often quoted as if it were the same thing.

In 2016 Martin Hagger and colleagues ran a pre-registered replication of the classic ego-depletion effect in 23 labs with 2,141 participants. The pooled effect was d = 0.04, with a 95% confidence interval from -0.07 to 0.15: close to zero, and the interval includes zero.

Keep the two apart. The vigilance decrement is a robust finding about performance on long watching tasks; what causes it is contested. Ego depletion is a claim about self-control carrying over between tasks, and it failed a large replication. One does not prop up or knock down the other. If someone tells you "willpower is a battery, so your focus runs out after lunch", they have merged two different claims, and the stronger-sounding half is the one that failed.

Real world

The jobs that watch for rare events. A lifeguard scanning a busy pool, a radiographer reading a long list of scans, a security screener looking at bag after bag: each spends hours watching for something that rarely appears, and each is the textbook setting for the decrement. Picture a team that rotates people between positions and builds in short changes of task: that design happens to suit all three explanations at once: some rest, an interruption to drift, and a fresh reminder of what they are looking for.

Part 3 · covers attention restoration theory

Does a walk in the park restore attention?

4 min live

Stephen Kaplan's attention restoration theory, set out in 1995 and reported here as the origin (the paper was not read), holds that directed attention tires with use, and that natural settings restore it because they hold attention gently, without effort. It is a pleasant idea and easy to overclaim. Here is what four sources in the line actually say.

LiveOne experiment, two reviews3 min▶

The experiment. Marc Berman, John Jonides and Stephen Kaplan reported in 2008 that a walk in nature, or even looking at pictures of nature, improved performance on backwards digit span (repeating a string of digits in reverse) and on the Attention Network Test from session 1, compared with urban walks or urban pictures.

The first review. Heather Ohly and colleagues pooled 31 studies in 2016. Of the meta-analyses they could run, three measures showed significant pooled effects (digit span forward, digit span backward and Trail Making B); the other ten did not. They flagged small samples and a lot of variation between studies.

The second review. Matt Stevenson, Theresa Schilhab and Peter Bentsen looked at 42 articles published since July 2013. Working memory and cognitive flexibility improved after nature exposure; attentional control, the part the theory is most about, was less reliable. Effects were low to moderate. Real exposures may do more than virtual ones, but real exposures also tended to last longer, so the two cannot be separated.

Put together: there is some support, it is uncertain which aspect of attention benefits, and the size is modest. That is a disagreement to teach, not one to resolve.

Self-studyWhat a desk worker can safely take2 min▶

A short break spent looking out of a window, or a few minutes outdoors, costs little and may help. The evidence does not promise a reset, and it does not say how long or how green the break has to be. Treat it as a cheap bet you can test on yourself, not a cure.

It also fits Part 2 neatly. A walk outside is rest (depletion), a change of scene (mind-wandering) and, if you use the last minute of it to remember what you are going back to, a goal refresh (habituation). Tomas's office has one window, at the end of the walkway; his plan uses it.

Build-along

An afternoon planned around the drift

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Tomas, still constructed, used to plan his afternoon as one solid stretch: 13:00 to 17:00 on the migration check, door shut, chat on mute, and a slow slide after the first hour. His notes from sessions 1 and 2 suggest his own drift starts somewhere around an hour into a monotonous task. So he redrew the afternoon as blocks shorter than that, with a small break between each, and moved the hardest thinking to the start. All of his times and estimates below are constructed. Build your own version alongside.

Find your longest monotonous task. Not the hardest one: the one that is long, quiet and repetitive, where nothing much happens and you are supposed to catch the one thing that is wrong. That is where the decrement lives.

★ Step 1 · the taskTomas: checking the migrated orders table row by row against the old system (about 3 hours, constructed) Mine: ______________________________________________

Estimate where your own drift starts. Use the session 1 diary (when did drift rows begin to appear?) and the session 2 probes (when did "off task" answers cluster?). A rough number is enough; you will test it on session 4's bench and in session 6.

★ Step 2 · my drift estimateDiary: drift rows start about __ min into a quiet task Probes: "off task" answers cluster after __ min Tomas: about 60 min on a monotonous task (constructed)

Cut the task into blocks shorter than that. If your drift starts at about an hour, a block of around fifty minutes keeps you out of the worst of it; if it starts at forty, try thirty to thirty-five. No study fixes one best interval; pick a length from your own estimate and treat it as a guess to check.

★ Step 3 · block lengthdrift estimate: __ min block length: shorter than that = __ min Tomas: drift ~60 -> blocks of 50, with the long check split in two

Put a goal-switch break between blocks. Design it to suit all three explanations from Part 2: a little rest, a change of scene, and a deliberate reminder of the goal. Two to five minutes is plenty to try.

★ Step 4 · the break1. stand up (rest) 2. look out of a window or step out (change of scene) 3. say where you are going next: "next block: rows 4,000 to 6,000, looking for null dates" (goal refresh)

Put the hardest work early. The first block of the afternoon is the one with the least time on task behind it. Spend it on the work that needs the most thought, and give the monotonous check the middle blocks, where the breaks protect it.

★ Step 5 · order13:00 hardest: pipeline design (thinking) 14:00 migration check, part 1 (watching) 15:00 migration check, part 2 (watching) 16:00 tickets & chat (light) Tomas's order, constructed

Write one prediction to test on the bench. Session 4 runs a go/no-go task at several block lengths on a modelled pool and on you. Write down what you expect so you can check it, rather than reading the result as if you knew it all along.

★ Step 6 · my prediction"If I do a short block and a longer block, my errors in the last part of the longer block will be ____ than in the short one." For reference, session 4's modelled pool drifts 4.3 points at 3 minutes and 8.4 at 10 (modelled, not a measurement of you).

The plan is a guess, not a rule. Tomas's 50-minute blocks are an estimate from two weeks of his own notes, all constructed. Your number will be different, and it will move with sleep, workload and the room. Session 6 turns this into a two-week experiment with a stop rule, so you find out whether it works for you instead of assuming it does.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What did Warm, Parasuraman and Matthews conclude in their 2008 review of vigilance?

The review drew on task type, workload, brain blood flow and stress reports, and found watching for rare events is hard work and stressful. B is the older reading it argued against. Nothing in the abstract limits the finding to any clinical group.

2 · In Ariga and Lleras's 2011 study, people who briefly switched to recalling memorised digits during a long task did not show the decrement. Why does that matter?

A pure depletion account predicts extra work should not help. Ariga and Lleras argued for goal habituation instead, but Thomson and colleagues' 2015 review still found no single theory explains everything. Nothing is settled, which is why a break that rests, re-engages and refreshes the goal is the safe design.

3 · A colleague says: "Willpower is a battery. Ego depletion failed to replicate, so the whole idea that focus fades over an hour is debunked." What is accurate?

Hagger and colleagues' 2016 replication tested self-control carrying over between tasks and found an effect close to zero. The decrement is about performance on long watching tasks and has been seen for decades. They are different claims; the failure of one says nothing about the other.
