Part 1 · covers phone presence

The phone on the desk, and the replication

6 min live

In 2017 a paper called "Brain drain" reported that the mere presence of your own phone, face down and silent, reduced the mental capacity you had available, even when you never touched it. It became one of the most quoted findings about phones. Five years later a pre-registered direct replication ran the same experiment and did not find it. Both results are real. Here is how to hold them at once.

LiveWhat "brain drain" claimed2 min▶

Adrian Ward, Kristen Duke, Ayelet Gneezy and Maarten Bos ran two experiments. According to the published summary, the mere presence of participants' own phones reduced their available cognitive capacity, and it did so even when they did not check the phone. The cost was highest for the people most dependent on their smartphones. The mechanism they proposed is attractive: some part of you is spending effort not attending to the phone.

University news coverage of the paper reported nearly 800 participants, and reported that people whose phone was in another room did better than people whose phone sat on the desk. Those two details come from the coverage, not from the abstract, so they stay "reported" here.

LiveWhat the replication found2 min▶

Ruiz Pardo and Minda (2022) ran a pre-registered direct replication of Ward's second experiment. Pre-registered means the method and the analysis were written down and published before the data came in, so nobody could choose the flattering test afterwards. They used six conditions: phone on the desk, in a pocket or bag, or outside the room, each with the phone switched on or off. Capacity was measured with an operation span task (OSpan, a working memory test) and a go/no-go task. The brain drain effect did not replicate.

A separate study, Hartmann and colleagues (2020), looked at memory: phone presence had no overall effect on short-term memory or on prospective memory (remembering to do something later). They did find a moderating effect for prospective memory in participants with low smartphone dependency, which is a narrower and stranger finding than the headline.

Self-studyHolding two results at once3 min▶

One failed replication does not prove the original wrong, and one original does not survive a careful failure untouched. The honest summary today is: the strong claim, "a silent phone on the desk drains your brain", has not held up in the replication designed to test it most directly, and the memory study found no overall effect either. The question is open.

So why does tonight's build-along still move the phone out of reach? Because the decision does not need the effect to be proven. Moving the phone costs nothing: no money, no lost information, a few seconds. If the effect is real, you gain; if it is not, you lose nothing. That is a cheap bet, and calling it a cheap bet rather than a proven fix keeps you honest when a colleague asks why you do it.

Ward, A. F., Duke, K., Gneezy, A., and Bos, M. W. (2017). Brain drain. Journal of the Association for Consumer Research, 2(2), 140-154.
Ruiz Pardo, A. C., and Minda, J. P. (2022). Reexamining the "brain drain" effect. Acta Psychologica, 230, 103717.
Hartmann, M., Martarelli, C. S., Reber, T. P., and Rothen, N. (2020). Does a smartphone on the desk drain our brain? Consciousness and Cognition, 86, 103033.

The one-line answer. "The original study found it, a pre-registered replication did not, so nobody knows yet. Putting the phone in a bag costs me nothing, so I do it as a cheap bet, not because it is proven."

Part 2 · covers notifications

A week with the alerts off

5 min live

Presence is contested. Alerts are a different thing: a phone that buzzes is not sitting quietly, it is asking for you. Two studies look at alerts from opposite ends, one in a lab over minutes, one in daily life over two weeks, and the second is worth reading line by line because it teaches how to read a small effect.

LiveAlerts in the lab: Stothart 20151 min▶

Cary Stothart, Ainsley Mitchum and Courtney Yehnert gave people an attention-demanding task and sent some of them phone notifications during it. Nobody answered or touched the phone. According to the abstract, receiving the notification alone disrupted performance, to a degree comparable with people who actively used their phones. The authors proposed the route runs through task-irrelevant thought: the buzz starts a train of thought about the message, and that train runs while you are supposed to be working. Session 2 met the same paper from the mind-wandering side.

LiveAlerts in daily life: Kushlev 20163 min▶

Kostadin Kushlev, Jason Proulx and Elizabeth Dunn recruited 221 University of British Columbia undergraduates from the general participant pool. Each person did both conditions, which is what within-subjects means: one week with alerts on and the phone kept within reach, and one week on Do Not Disturb with the phone kept out of sight. The order was counterbalanced, so half did the alerts-on week first and half did it second, which stops "the second week was just calmer" from explaining the result.

At the end of each week people rated their own inattention and hyperactivity symptoms. Inattention averaged 2.38 in the alerts-on week and 2.27 in the alerts-off week (SD .58 and .56), t(189) = 3.02, p = .003, d = .44. Hyperactivity went from 2.11 to 2.01, d = .45. Higher inattention went with lower productivity and lower well-being.

Self-studyReading 0.11 next to d = .443 min▶

Two numbers describe the same result and they feel very different. The raw difference is 0.11 on the rating scale, about a tenth of a point. The effect size is d = .44, which sounds respectable. Both are true. The effect size is larger than the raw gap suggests because each person was compared with themselves: their own two weeks moved together, so a consistent small shift stands out clearly against the variation inside each person.

The honest reading has four parts, and you need all of them. It was within-subjects, so the comparison is strong. It was self-report, so it measures how attentive people felt, not a test score. The raw change is small. And the sample was ordinary students, so these are symptoms on a scale, in a non-clinical group, not ADHD and not a diagnosis. Read that way, the study says: turning alerts off for a week made people feel a little less scattered, reliably.

The modelled bench in session 4 pointed the same way from a different angle: in its 10-minute block, banners raised the last-fifth commission rate from 31.4 to 35.8 percent. That is a model built to illustrate, not a measurement, but it shows the mechanism you are designing against.

Stothart, C., Mitchum, A., and Yehnert, C. (2015). The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification. JEP: Human Perception and Performance, 41(4), 893-897.
Kushlev, K., Proulx, J., and Dunn, E. W. (2016). "Silence your phones": Smartphone notifications increase inattention and hyperactivity symptoms. Proceedings of CHI 2016, 1011-1020.

Real world

What the UBC students actually did. They did not delete apps or go offline. For one week the phone stayed on Do Not Disturb and out of sight; messages still arrived and could be read whenever they chose to look. The change was who decided when: the phone or the person. That is the change tonight's build-along makes, at a smaller scale, for your focus block only.

When it is more than settings. If inattention or restlessness is persistent and long-standing, that is a conversation with a doctor, not a settings change.

Part 3 · covers the room

Signals, not willpower

4 min live

The room is where Tomas loses the most and where the evidence is thinnest. Everything in this Part is practice: habits that working teams use and that cost little to try, not findings with measured effects. They share one idea. A signal works before the choice arrives: a colleague who sees headphones and a status saying "back at 11:00" does not walk over, so you never have to decide whether to look up.

LiveSix signals that do the refusing3 min▶

Face away from the walkway. Session 1 showed orienting moves your eyes before you decide anything. If people pass in your line of sight, every pass is a small orienting event. Turning the desk, or your chair, removes the events instead of resisting them.

Headphones as a visible signal. In an open-plan room, headphones say "in the middle of something" to anyone deciding whether to walk over. Their value is as much the signal as the sound.

A status message with a return time. "Focus block, back at 11:00" answers the colleague's real question, which is when, not whether. A return time makes waiting easy; a bare "busy" makes people try anyway.

Batch chat to set times. Check on the hour, or at the end of each block, instead of whenever a badge appears. The messages still get read; the timing becomes yours.

A shared team agreement on response times. Personal settings fail if the team expects replies in two minutes. One line agreed together, such as "chat within the hour, call if it is urgent", makes everyone's focus block legitimate.

Notification tiers. A short list of people and systems who can break through Do Not Disturb, and everyone else waits. The build-along makes this list.

Self-studyWhere the other pieces live2 min▶

What each interruption costs once it lands, and how long getting back takes, is taught with its evidence in Time Management session 5 ↗. Tonight is about stopping the interruption before it lands.

If your batch bin holds dozens of messages an hour, sorting them by hand at each check becomes its own job. Using AI to triage messages, summarise threads and flag what needs you is covered in AI Productivity ↗. The two fit together: settings decide when messages reach you, triage decides what you read first when they do.

Self-studyA team that agreed one line1 min▶

Imagine a hypothetical analytics team where everyone answers chat within minutes because everyone else does. Nobody wants it; nobody can stop first. One person proposes a line at stand-up: "mornings until 11:00 are quiet, chat is read at 11:00, call for production incidents". The change costs nothing, and it works only because it is shared: one person going quiet alone looks unresponsive, the whole team going quiet together looks like a policy.

Build-along

The notification audit and the desk plan

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Get your phone out and open your chat app's settings. Tomas, who is constructed, did this audit on one ordinary day: he counted 14 apps that could alert him and kept only 3 able to break through his focus block. His lists below are invented for teaching; yours come from your own phone. Keep what you write on paper or in your own notes; it stays with you.

List every app that can alert you, with a count. Your phone's notification settings list them; add your laptop's chat and email. Next to each, write roughly how many alerts it sent yesterday, or keep a tally tomorrow. Guesses are fine for a first pass.

★ Step 1 · Tomas's list (constructed)app | alerts in one day ----------------+------------------ team chat | 38 email | 22 calendar | 6 ticket system | 9 on-call alerts | 1 Priya (DM) | 4 family group | 3 news, social, shopping + 4 more | 31

Sort into three bins. Ask one question per app: does this need to reach me in the next hour, or can it wait? Break through is for the few that genuinely cannot wait. Batch is for work that matters but can arrive in one sitting. Off is for everything that never needed a sound.

★ Step 2 · the three bins (constructed)BREAK THROUGH (3) : on-call alerts, Priya, family group BATCH (4) : team chat, email, calendar, tickets OFF (7) : news, social, shopping, and 4 more Test for the top bin: would I leave a meeting for it?

Set a Do Not Disturb schedule for your focus block. Use your phone's focus or Do Not Disturb mode, and the same in your chat app. Add the break-through list as the allowed exceptions. Schedule it, so it switches on without a decision.

★ Step 3 · the schedule (constructed)Focus block : weekdays 09:00 to 11:00 Phone : Do Not Disturb, scheduled Allowed : on-call alerts, Priya, family group Chat app : pause notifications 09:00 to 11:00 Laptop : banners off, badges off

Move the phone out of reach for the block. A bag, a drawer, another room. Label it honestly in your notes: the 2017 study reported a benefit and the 2022 replication did not find one, so this is a cheap bet, not a proven fix. It costs a few seconds.

★ Step 4 · where the phone goesDuring the block : in my bag, under the desk Why : costs nothing; evidence is mixed (2017 reported a benefit, 2022 replication did not) Break-through : still rings, because DND lets it

Write a chat status template with a return time. Make it reusable, so setting it takes one click. Include when you are back and how to reach you if it truly cannot wait.

★ Step 5 · status template🎧 Focus block until 11:00. Reading chat at 11:00. Production down? Call me. Tomas's desk plan: chair turned away from the walkway, headphones on, phone in bag, status set.

Draft a one-line team agreement. Not a policy document: one line you could read out at stand-up. Name the response expectation and the urgent route. You will ask one teammate about it this week.

★ Step 6 · agreement draft (constructed)Draft for Priya, Marco and Ana: "Chat is answered within the hour, not the minute. If it cannot wait an hour, call."

Keep the top bin short. Every name you add to break through is a door left open during the block. If the list grows past a handful, ask of each one: would I leave a meeting for this? If not, it belongs in batch.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Run one day with the new settings and one day without. At the end of each, write two lines on how the day felt. No score, no rating to compare; this is a note to yourself.
  2. Count the alerts that would have broken through. On the settings day, how many times did something in your break-through bin actually ring? If it was zero, the list may be longer than it needs to be.
  3. Ask one teammate about the agreement. Read them your one-line draft and write down what they would change.
  4. Bring your best block's setting to session 6. Which block felt longest and least broken, and what was set: DND, phone place, status, desk direction?
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A colleague says "studies prove a phone on your desk drains your brain, even switched off". What is the most accurate reply?

Ruiz Pardo and Minda (2022) ran six conditions, including on and off, and the effect did not replicate. B overreaches the other way: a failed replication leaves the question open, it does not prove the opposite. D invents a confirmation the replication did not give.

2 · Kushlev and colleagues (2016) found inattention of 2.38 with alerts on and 2.27 with alerts off, d = .44. Which reading is accurate?

The design was within-subjects and counterbalanced, which rules out D. B turns symptom ratings in ordinary students into a diagnosis and a treatment. C ignores that a small, consistent shift within each person can still be a reliable effect; the honest reading keeps the small raw gap and the effect size together.

3 · Why do room signals such as a turned desk, headphones and a status with a return time work better than resolving to ignore interruptions?

A signal acts before the interruption: the colleague does not walk over, the passer-by is out of view. A is false: these are practices, not measured findings. C leans on ego depletion, which failed a large replication (session 3). D confuses a signal with a rule; it works because people read it, not because it is enforced.
