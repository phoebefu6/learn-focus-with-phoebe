The attention bench

Three sessions of reading about attention; tonight it gets measured, on you. The bench runs a sustained-attention task built on the one Ian Robertson and colleagues published in 1997: a stream of digits, one a little over every second, and you press for every digit except 3. It is boring on purpose, because boredom is where sustained attention shows. You run it four ways: plain, with chat banners sliding in, after a two-minute break, and with the banners muted. The page clocks every press and prints your own numbers per block. Then a modelled pool of 200 invented people shows what one short block cannot: how drift grows with time on task, what banners add, and how badly a too-short measurement disagrees with itself.

What he does not have is a number taken under controlled conditions: the same task, the same timing, with one thing changed at a time. The bench gives him that, and gives it to you. Part 1 · covers the go/no-go task and its four readings

How a digit becomes four numbers

7 min live

The task follows the timing used by Paul Seli and colleagues in 2013: each digit shows for 250 milliseconds, then a circled x covers it for 900 milliseconds, so a trial lasts 1150 milliseconds whether you press or not. Digits come in shuffled runs of nine, so one trial in nine is a 3, and they appear in five sizes so you cannot respond to shape alone. Press for every digit that is not a 3; hold back on the 3.

LiveWhat each reading means3 min▶

Commission errors are the headline reading of the task: pressing on a 3 because the rhythm carried your hand. Robertson and colleagues designed the task to produce exactly these "slips of action", and found that, in 75 healthy controls and 34 people with brain injury, the slips tracked self- and informant-reported everyday attention failures. They also found that false presses were preceded by a shortening of reaction times: the hand speeds up just before it slips.

Mean reaction time on the go trials is how fast you pressed. RT variability, the standard deviation divided by the mean, is how uneven your rhythm was; Seli, Cheyne and Smilek found response variability rose in the trials before people reported their mind had wandered. Omission errors are go trials you missed altogether, the reading that rises when attention lapses completely rather than running on autopilot.

LiveNever read errors without speed3 min▶

Seli, Jonker, Cheyne and Smilek (2013) showed that the task is open to a speed-accuracy trade-off. In their 108 participants, error rate and reaction time correlated at r = 0.77: people who pressed slower made fewer commission errors. When people were told to go slowly, they made 16 percent fewer errors, and the whole difference was accounted for by their slower pressing.

That is why the bench prints mean reaction time next to every error count, and why its notes compare speed whenever errors change. If your banner block shows fewer errors and a slower reaction time, you did not necessarily attend better; you may have pressed more cautiously. Part 4 comes back to what this means for any attention test you meet at work.

Build-along · the bench, part 1

Four steps, measured on you

★ 16 min · everyone runs it

Each block is 3 minutes: 157 trials, of which 17 or 18 are a 3. Run the steps in order. Use the space bar on a keyboard or the big button on a touch screen, and keep the same hand and posture throughout so the blocks are comparable. During block 2, chat banners from Tomas's invented team slide in at the top of the task; do not answer them, keep pressing. The break is real time: look at something far away, stand up if you can.

Read your table in this order. Speed first, then errors, then variability. If two blocks differ by two commission errors or fewer out of 17, chance alone produces that, so say "no clear difference". If an error count fell and reaction time rose, you may have traded speed for accuracy. Then switch the block length to 30 seconds and run block 1 again: three or so no-go trials, and one slip moves the rate by more than 30 points. That is the break button, on you.

Part 2 · covers the modelled pool and time on task

What one block cannot show: drift over time

8 min live

Your three blocks are three minutes each, in a fixed order, from one person. To see how performance drifts with time on task, and what banners add when nothing else changes, the bench runs a modelled pool: 200 synthetic participants, each with a starting speed, variability and error tendency, drifting with minutes on task by a rule printed on the widget. It is a model, fitted to nobody; only the direction of each change is the claim. An independent Python run reproduces every number below exactly.

LiveReading the pool3 min▶

Start on the 10-minute block: commission rate 23.0 percent in the first fifth of trials and 31.4 percent in the last, a drift of 8.4 points, with RT variability rising from 0.22 to 0.25. The 3-minute block, the length you ran, drifts by 4.3 points (20.9 to 25.3 percent); 20 minutes drifts by 10.9 (24.5 to 35.5 percent). That shape, a decline over time on task that is steepest early, is the vigilance decrement session 3 described; here it is built into the model on purpose, so you can see what a measurement of it looks like.

Then the break: two 5-minute blocks with a 2-minute break between them, the same ten minutes of task. The model gives back most of the drift at the break, so the last fifth ends at 28.6 percent against 31.4 percent straight through. Session 6 builds a focus block around exactly this, and session 3 explains why the reason a break helps is still argued over.

Self-studyThe rule behind the pool3 min▶

Each synthetic person gets a starting reaction time, variability, commission tendency and a susceptibility s, drawn with a fixed seed. With t minutes on task, drift is f = s t / (t + 6). On a 3, the chance of pressing is the starting tendency plus 0.30 f; a missed press on other digits starts at 1 percent and rises with f; reaction time speeds slightly and its variability grows with f. A banner disrupts the next four trials. A 2-minute break gives back 80 percent of the time on task. Every setting runs twice with fresh randomness.

None of these settings is fitted to data. They were chosen so the pool behaves in the directions the vigilance literature describes and so the anti-lever and the break button have something to show. Use the pool to compare settings with each other, never to predict your own numbers.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever and the break button

What unanswered banners cost, and when a test measures nobody

6 min live

Two settings on the pool exist to make the bench report something worse. One is a habit most offices treat as harmless. The other breaks the measurement itself, which is how you know the measurement was doing something before.

LiveThe anti-lever: banners on, never answered3 min▶

"I leave chat open but I don't answer it" is how many people describe their discipline. The model: a banner every 12 to 28 seconds disrupts the next four trials, raising the chance of a slip and the unevenness of responses. Over 10 minutes the last-fifth commission rate rises from 31.4 to 35.8 percent, the first fifth from 23.0 to 26.6, and missed presses in the last fifth from 3.8 to 6.1 percent. No banner was ever answered.

The honest nuance: the size is a setting, not a finding. What the model encodes is a direction that research supports: Stothart, Mitchum and Yehnert (2015) found that notifications alone, with no interaction with the phone, disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task, comparable in size to actively using the phone, and proposed that notifications prompt task-irrelevant thought. Session 5 reads that study next to the phone-presence studies that did not replicate.

LiveThe break button: a 30-second task3 min▶

A bench that can only produce sensible numbers is measuring nothing. Shorten the task to 30 seconds: 26 trials, about three of them a 3. In that time the model builds in a drift of only 1.0 point, yet the measure reports 4.6, because with half a no-go trial in each window the rate jumps on single presses. Run the same 200 people twice and their commission rates agree at r = 0.11, against 0.41 for 3 minutes, 0.73 for 10 and 0.85 for 20.

The decrement vanishes because there is no time on task for it to happen in, and the measure becomes useless because there are too few chances to slip. Both failures show up in the quick "attention tests" that circulate online. Try it on yourself with the block-length switch above: run block 1 at 30 seconds twice and compare.

Part 4 · covers what the task can and cannot tell you

A good instrument, read narrowly

5 min live

The task has a respectable record: Robertson and colleagues found its slips tracked everyday attention failures and other sustained-attention tests, not other kinds of attention. It also has a known weakness, the speed-accuracy trade-off, and a hard limit, the number of no-go trials. Reading it well means holding all three.

LiveThree rules for any attention test3 min▶

Speed next to errors. Seli and colleagues' r = 0.77 between error rate and reaction time means an error count alone mixes attention with pace. Compare blocks at similar speeds, or say plainly that pace changed.

Enough chances to fail. A rate built on 17 no-go trials moves about 6 points per slip; on 3 it moves more than 30. The model's run-twice agreement, 0.41 at 3 minutes and 0.11 at 30 seconds, is the same point at the level of a group.

One sitting is one sitting. Sleep, caffeine, the time of day, the device and practice all move these numbers. A difference between your blocks tonight is a fact about tonight. That is why session 6 judges a focus routine over two weeks, not one afternoon.

Self-studyWhy no "normal range" is printed here2 min▶

Published studies report group averages for their own versions of the task, with their own timing, number of trials, instructions, screens and participants. None of those averages was read at source for this page, and a number from a different version would invite exactly the comparison the bench warns against: you against a stranger, on a different instrument, on a different day. Compare your blocks with each other; that comparison holds the person, the device and the evening constant.

Real world

The five-minute focus quiz. Apps and articles offer quick "attention tests" that return a score in under a minute or two. Read them with this page's three rules: is speed shown next to errors, how many chances to fail were there, and was it one sitting? A 30-second test with a handful of targets will rank the same person differently each time, which the break button showed on 200 modelled people.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · In your banner block you made 2 fewer commission errors than at baseline, and your mean reaction time was 60 ms slower. What is the best reading?

Seli and colleagues found errors and reaction time correlated at r = 0.77, and slowing down cut errors by 16 percent. With 17 no-go trials, two errors is noise. The honest reading is "no clear difference, and I changed my pace".

2 · On the modelled pool, a 30-second task shows a measured drift of 4.6 points while the model only built in 1.0, and two runs of the same people agree at r = 0.11. What does that show?

That is the break button working as intended: it proves the bench measures something at 3 minutes and more (agreement 0.41, 0.73, 0.85) by showing the measure falling apart when the conditions for measuring are removed.

3 · Over 10 modelled minutes, unanswered banners raise the last-fifth commission rate from 31.4 to 35.8 percent. How should that be stated?
