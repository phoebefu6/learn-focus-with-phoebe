Learn Focus with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

What attention is, and what it is not

"I can't focus" sounds like one problem. It is usually three. Something has to get you ready, something has to point you at the right thing, and something has to decide, again and again, that the query you are writing beats the message that just arrived. Tonight we separate those three jobs, follow the most quoted number about attention back to where it came from, clear away what attention is not, and keep a diary of one working morning so the next five sessions have your own material to work on.

Part 0

Where we start

The running case is constructed. Tomas is an invented data engineer in an open-plan office. His team chat is open all day, his desk faces the walkway to the kitchen, and the work that matters most, designing and debugging data pipelines, needs forty uninterrupted minutes he rarely gets. He is not unusual and nothing is wrong with him. Across six sessions he learns what his attention is doing, measures it, and changes the setting around it. Every number about Tomas is invented for teaching; every number about research is cited.

Part 1 · covers the three attention networks

Three jobs, not one span

6 min live

Michael Posner and Steven Petersen proposed in 1990 that attention is not one faculty but a system of separate networks, and updated the framework in 2012. The three functions most people now use are alerting, orienting and executive control. Fan and colleagues built a 30-minute test that measures all three at once; in 40 adults, the three efficiencies were uncorrelated, which is the point: one can be in good shape while another struggles.

LiveAlerting, orienting, executive control3 min▶

Alerting is reaching and holding a ready state. A warning that something is about to happen speeds the response to it; that is why a ping works so well on you even when you meant to ignore it. It also has a slow side: the readiness you can hold across a long task, which is session 3's subject.

Orienting is selecting where to point the senses: a location, a voice, a corner of the screen. Often the eyes move first and the decision follows. When Tomas's colleague walks past to the kitchen, his orienting system has turned his head before he has formed the thought "I should keep working".

Executive control is resolving conflict between possible responses: keep typing the query, or switch to the chat. Fan and colleagues measured it with flanker arrows pointing the wrong way around a target. It is the job most people mean when they say "focus", and the one most exposed to a day full of small competing claims.

Self-studyWhat the 1990 and 2012 papers do and do not say3 min▶

The 1990 article proposed the system; it has no abstract online and is cited here as the origin, not quoted. The 2012 update, "20 years after", says the framework has been elaborated and expanded: research on orienting and executive functions added further networks of brain regions, development studies showed control systems changing between infancy and childhood, and some individual differences in network efficiency were linked to specific genetic variations together with experience.

None of that is needed to use the idea at work. What carries over is the separation: a tired morning can leave orienting intact (you notice everything) while executive control is low (you act on everything you notice). Fan and colleagues' finding that the three efficiencies were uncorrelated, with some interactions, is the evidence that the split is more than a teaching device.

Real world

The air traffic controller and the analyst. A controller watching for a rare event leans on alerting held over hours; an analyst reading a dense table leans on executive control holding one line of thought against the next tempting one. Both say "focus". They need different protection: the first needs breaks against a fading ready state, the second needs fewer competing claims. Sessions 3 and 5 take them in turn.

Part 2 · covers the goldfish statistic, traced

The eight-second goldfish

5 min live

You have probably heard that the human attention span fell from 12 seconds in 2000 to 8 seconds, one second less than a goldfish. In May 2015 TIME ran it as a headline, crediting a Microsoft study. The trail behind the number is short, and it ends nowhere.

LiveWhat the trail shows3 min▶

A 2015 report by Microsoft Canada's consumer insights team, written for advertisers, printed the 12, 8 and 9 second figures and credited them to a website called Statistic Brain (reported by the Wall Street Journal and the BBC in 2017; the report itself was not opened for this page). The report's own research, a survey of 2,000 Canadians and EEG recordings of 112 people, was a different study and did not produce those three numbers.

In 2017 Jo Craven McGinty at the Wall Street Journal and Simon Maybin at the BBC followed the citation. Statistic Brain listed the US National Library of Medicine and the Associated Press as its sources. A senior researcher at the library found no reference to the statistics; the Associated Press could not find an article with them; Statistic Brain did not answer emails or calls. Edward Vogel, who had measured students' attention for 20 years, told the Journal it had been "remarkably stable across decades". The BBC also asked a fish behaviour researcher: there is no evidence goldfish have particularly short attention spans either.

Self-studyWhy a span in seconds cannot exist2 min▶

Gemma Briggs, an attention researcher at the Open University, told the BBC that attention is "very much task-dependent": how much attention people apply depends on what the task demands and on what they bring to it. A single number in seconds would have to be the same for a film, a spreadsheet, a conversation and a drive. Researchers measure specific things instead: how fast you respond after a warning, how often you miss a rare target over twenty minutes, how much a distractor slows you. Session 4 measures some of those on you, and none of them is a span.

There is a real question underneath the myth: has heavy phone use changed how people allocate attention? That is open and actively studied; session 5 reads what the phone studies found and where they failed to replicate. "Shorter than a goldfish" is not an answer to it.

The one-line answer. "That number traces to a marketing report that credited a website nobody could get a source from. Attention is not one span; it depends on the task and the setting, and the setting is the part we can change."

Part 3 · covers what attention is not

Not a span, not a trait, not a multitasking engine

4 min live

Clearing three wrong models now saves time later, because each one points at the wrong fix. If attention were a fixed span, nothing could be done. If it were a personal trait, the fix would be willpower. If it could run two demanding streams at once, chat would be free. None of the three holds.

LiveThree things attention is not3 min▶

Not a span. Part 2 dealt with that. What exists instead are measurable performances on particular tasks, which change with time on task, with what else is competing, and with the state you arrive in.

Not a fixed trait you can read off one sitting. People do differ, and some differences are stable. But one short measurement says more about that sitting than about the person; session 4 shows, on a modelled pool, how much a 3-minute measurement disagrees with itself when the same people are run twice.

Not a multitasking engine. Two demanding streams of thought do not run side by side; the mind alternates, and alternating has a cost. That cost, measured in the lab and in offices, is taught in Time Management session 5 ↗. Here it is enough to know that "I'll keep chat open and still think" is a choice to alternate.

Self-studyWhen it is more than everyday focus2 min▶

Everything in these six sessions assumes ordinary variation: some mornings are better than others, some rooms are worse. Concentration problems that are persistent, that have lasted since childhood, that are getting worse, or that come with low mood, exhaustion, poor sleep or memory lapses deserve a conversation with a doctor rather than a better calendar. A focus routine is not a substitute for that conversation, and nothing here can tell the two apart.

Build-along

The attention diary, one working morning

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You cannot fix a morning you only remember as "bitty". Tomas kept a diary of one morning, writing a line each time his attention moved, and tagged each line with the job involved and what moved it. Build the template now, fill in this morning from memory as practice, and keep tomorrow's morning live. The entries below are Tomas's, constructed.

Set up five columns. Paper, a note app or a spreadsheet, whichever you will actually open. One row per move of attention, not per task.

★ Step 1 · the columnstime | what I was doing | what moved me | which job | back in after -----+-------------------+--------------+-----------+------------- | | | |

Log the morning as it happened. Write a row each time your attention moves, whether something outside moved it or you drifted on your own. Tomas's first rows:

★ Step 2 · Tomas's rows (constructed)09:00 | writing the orders query | start | control | - 09:40 | writing the orders query | chat ping | alerting, then orienting | 23 min 10:05 | debugging the load | (back in) | control | - 10:12 | debugging the load | chat ping | alerting | 2 min 10:38 | debugging the load | Ana at my desk | orienting | 6 min

Tag the job that failed or was spent. Use Part 1's three: alerting (a warning grabbed you), orienting (your eyes or ears went somewhere), executive control (you chose the other thing). Most rows carry two; write the first.

★ Step 3 · tagsalerting = a sound, buzz or banner got me ready, uninvited orienting = my eyes or ears went to a place control = I chose the other thing, or could not choose drift = nothing outside happened; I was elsewhere (session 2)

Note how long getting back took. A guess is fine. You are not measuring switching costs, which time management covers; you are spotting which pulls turn into threads.

★ Step 4 · back in after2 min = the ping was read and dropped 23 min = the ping became a thread (Tomas, 09:40) never = the block ended there

Find your longest unbroken stretch, and why it was unbroken. Tomas's was the last hour, headphones on, because the reviewer was waiting. That reason is a design clue for session 5.

★ Step 5 · the longest stretchLongest: 11:00 to 12:00, code review, 60 min Why: headphones on, chat minimised, someone waiting on me Copy this setting to: the 09:00 block tomorrow

Count, then stop. Rows per hour, and how many were drift with nothing outside happening. Bring both counts to session 2.

★ Step 6 · the countsTomas: 9 rows in 3 hours, 2 of them drift Yours: __ rows in __ hours, __ of them drift

Keep the diary short. A diary that interrupts you more than the chat does has failed. One line, five fields, under ten seconds. If you miss rows, that is data too: it means the pull won before you noticed it.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A ping arrives while Tomas is writing a query. His head turns before he decides anything, and then he spends a minute choosing whether to reply. Which jobs of attention were involved, in order?

The ping raised readiness, the turn of the head selected a place, and the minute of choosing was conflict resolution. Fan and colleagues found the three networks' efficiencies uncorrelated, which is why it pays to name which one is struggling. B is the model Part 3 rejects.

2 · A colleague says "our attention span is now eight seconds, shorter than a goldfish". What is accurate?

Microsoft's own EEG study was a different piece of work that did not produce the headline numbers. D replaces one invented span with another: no study measures attention as a single span in seconds.

3 · Why does the model "attention is a capacity shaped by task, state and setting" lead to better fixes than "attention is a span"?

A span leaves nothing to do but blame the person. A capacity that tires and recovers, and is spent by competing claims, points at the setting, the block length and the breaks, which sessions 3, 5 and 6 work on. C is wrong: alternating has a cost however rested you are.
