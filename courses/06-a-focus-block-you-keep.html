Part 1 · covers deep work as a practice

Deep work is an argument, the block is a practice

5 min live

Cal Newport's book Deep Work (2016) argues that long stretches of distraction-free concentration on demanding tasks are both increasingly valuable and increasingly rare, and that they have to be scheduled and protected rather than hoped for. It is an argument, well made, drawing on examples and on research others did. It is not a study that measured blocks against no blocks, and nothing on this page quotes a number from it.

LiveWhat is argued, what is measured, what is practice3 min▶

Argued: concentrated work on hard problems produces more value per hour than fragmented work, and a working culture of constant availability erodes it (Newport, reported; an argument from cases).

Measured, in pieces: notifications alone disrupted an attention task (Stothart and colleagues, session 5); a week with alerts on raised self-reported inattention a little (Kushlev and colleagues, session 5); performance drifts with time on task and the reason is contested (session 3). No study here tested "a scheduled 90-minute block every morning" against "no block" on real work output.

Practice: claiming the time before the week fills, setting up the room first, a start ritual, a stop rule. These are reasonable designs assembled from the measured pieces, which is why the build-along tests them on you rather than promising a result.

Self-studyWhere calendar mechanics live2 min▶

How to defend a block against a full calendar, estimate how long work will take, and say no to the meeting that wants the slot are time-management questions, with their own bench; see Time Management ↗. Here the block is designed for attention: how long it runs, what happens at its edges, and how you will know it works for you.

Real world

The "focus time" status that nobody honours. A common pattern: a calendar tool offers to auto-book focus time, the team sees a wall of it, and meetings get booked over it anyway because "it is only focus time". A block works when the people around it treat it like a meeting with someone important. That is a team agreement, from session 5, not a calendar feature.

Part 2 · covers scheduled breaks and the 52-and-17 figure

Breaks on a schedule, breaks by choice

5 min live

Felicitas Biwer and colleagues (2023) asked 87 university students to study for one day under one of three break rules: take breaks whenever they chose, take a 6-minute break after every 24 minutes (a "Pomodoro" rhythm), or a 3-minute break after every 12. It is one of the few direct comparisons of scheduled and self-chosen breaks, and its result is narrower than either side of the argument usually claims.

LiveWhat the break study found, and did not3 min▶

Students who chose their own breaks studied in longer sessions and took longer breaks, and that went with more fatigue and distractedness and lower concentration and motivation than in the two scheduled groups. There was no difference between groups in invested mental effort or in task completion. The authors read it as mood benefits and apparent efficiency benefits for scheduled breaks: similar completion in shorter time.

What it does not show: that 25 minutes is special, that one rhythm beats another for everyone, or anything about a working day beyond one day of student self-study. It fits with session 3's goal-switch finding, Ariga and Lleras's brief task switches that averted a vigilance decrement, and with the modelled pool on the bench, where two 5-minute blocks with a break ended at 28.6 percent commission against 31.4 percent for ten straight minutes. Three different kinds of evidence point the same way; none of them fixes the interval.

Self-study"Work 52 minutes, rest 17"2 min▶

A widely shared ratio says the most productive people work for 52 minutes and break for 17. It is reported to come from a 2014 blog post by the time-tracking app DeskTime, describing patterns in its own users' data. It was not peer reviewed, and the post was not read for this page, so it is reported, not checked. Treat it the way session 1 treated the goldfish: a number with a story attached and no study under it. Pick an interval because it fits your work and your diary, then test it.

Make the break a real switch. Session 3's goal-habituation idea suggests the useful part of a break is stepping out of the task goal and back in. Standing up and looking out of a window is a switch. Opening the chat you muted for the block is a different task that may not end on time, which is how a 6-minute break becomes a 23-minute thread.

Part 3 · covers keeping the block, and judging it

Two weeks and a stop rule

5 min live

Session 4's break button showed that a 30-second test ranks the same people almost at random, and that even a 3-minute block agreed with itself at only 0.41 in the modelled pool. The same logic applies to a focus routine. One good afternoon proves nothing, one bad one disproves nothing. A routine is judged over enough days to see past the noise, against days without it, with the reading decided before you start.

LiveThree habits that keep a block alive3 min▶

A start ritual that sets up the room. The same three moves every time: phone into the bag, Do Not Disturb on with its break-through list, chat status set with a return time. Session 5's desk plan, turned into a sequence that takes under a minute. The ritual is also a cue: doing it says "the block has started" to you and to the people who can see your status.

A length you can actually hold, with a planned break. Start with the length your session 3 plan suggested, usually shorter than you hope, and a break that is a real switch. Lengthen only after a week where the end of the block still felt like the middle.

A stop rule written in advance. What would tell you the block is not working, and what you will do then: shorten it, move it to another time of day, or drop it. Written on day zero so that a bad Tuesday cannot rewrite it, and a good Thursday cannot either.

Self-studyWhy alternate days instead of before and after2 min▶

"Two weeks without, then two weeks with" mixes the block with everything else that changed: a deadline arriving, a holiday ending, practice at keeping a diary. Alternating block days and normal days in a pattern fixed in advance, and swapping the pattern in week two, spreads those changes across both kinds of day, the same reason Kushlev and colleagues swapped the order of their two weeks for half their participants. It is still one person and ten days, so read the result as "worth keeping" or "worth redesigning", never as proof.

Build-along

The block card and the two-week test

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Tomas (constructed) turns five sessions of notes into one card he keeps next to the keyboard, and a two-week plan with its rule written tonight. Write yours alongside his; every value on his card is invented for teaching, and yours should come from your own diary, probes and bench notes.

Name the one task the block is for. Not "deep work", a task with a noun. A block with no named task fills with whatever is loudest.

★ Step 1 · the taskTomas: the orders pipeline redesign (constructed) Mine: ______________________________

Choose length and break from your own evidence. Use your session 3 plan and your probe log: where did unaware drift start clustering? Start below that.

★ Step 2 · length and breakDrift showed up after about: 60 min (Tomas, probe log) Block: 50 min Break: 7 min, a real switch Then: one more block Mine: block ___ min, break ___ min, how many ___

Write the start ritual as three moves. Take them from session 5's desk plan and notification bins.

★ Step 3 · start ritual1. phone into the bag 2. Do Not Disturb on (break-through: my manager, on-call) 3. chat status: "heads down, back at 10:45"

Claim the slots before the week fills. Put the blocks in the calendar now, as appointments, on the days your two-week pattern says.

★ Step 4 · the patternWeek 1 block days: Mon, Wed, Fri normal: Tue, Thu Week 2 block days: Tue, Thu normal: Mon, Wed, Fri Window: 09:00 to 10:50 every day (blocks only on block days)

Pick two numbers and the stop rule. Diary rows per hour in the block window (from session 1) and a 1 to 5 end-of-day rating of the work that mattered. Write what result means keep, redesign or drop.

★ Step 5 · numbers and ruleDaily: rows/hour 09:00-10:50, rating 1-5 Keep: block days better on both, on most days Redesign: better on one only -> shorten to 35 min, retest Drop: no difference after redesign -> try afternoons

Tell one person. The team agreement from session 5, applied: one colleague who knows what your status means and will route urgent things through the break-through list.

★ Step 6 · one person"For two weeks I'm testing a focus block, 09:00 to 10:50 on the days in my calendar. Status says when I'm back. If it's urgent, call; chat waits till the break."

Expect the deadline week. The block that survives is the one with a rule for the bad week: keep the first 50 minutes even when the second has to go. A shorter block kept beats a perfect block cancelled.

Homework

The next two weeks

10 working days
  1. Run the two-week test exactly as written on your card, including the swapped pattern in week two.
  2. Log the two numbers every day, block days and normal days alike, in under a minute.
  3. Run the attention bench once in each week, at the same time of day, and note it next to the day's numbers without reading much into a single sitting.
  4. On day 10, apply the rule you wrote, not the one you wish you had written. Keep, redesign or drop, and write one sentence on why.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A colleague says "research proves a 90-minute deep-work block every morning makes you more productive". What is accurate?

No study here tested a daily block against no block on real output. C overcorrects: absence of a trial is not evidence against. D borrows a ratio that traces to an app's blog post.

2 · In Biwer and colleagues' 2023 study, what happened to students who chose their own breaks, compared with scheduled breaks?

The schedule changed how the day felt and how long it took, not how much got done, and the study did not crown one rhythm. It was 87 students and one day, so it is a reason to try a schedule, not a law.

3 · Why write the stop rule on day 0 and alternate block days with normal days?

Session 4 showed how noisy short measurements are. Alternating spreads other changes across both conditions, the reason Kushlev and colleagues swapped their week order; a rule written first stops you choosing the reading after seeing the result. A overclaims: ten days says keep or redesign, not proof.
