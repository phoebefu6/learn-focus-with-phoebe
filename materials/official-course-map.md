# learn-focus-with-phoebe - source map ("Getting Focused")

Bucket `move`, d2, audience everyone, single track of 6 sessions x 45 min. Built 2026-10-12.
Angle: attention as a capacity you can protect and train. Running case: **Tomas**, a fictional data
engineer in an open-plan office with team chat open all day (constructed; every number about Tomas
is invented for teaching and says so). Not medical advice: said on the landing page, session 1 and
on the bench.

Evidence tiers used below:
- **AS** read at source (the paper's own abstract or full text, or the institution's own page)
- **AS-abs** read at source, abstract only (no number from the body is quoted)
- **R** reported (secondary source; quote with "reported", or cut)
- **C** constructed (Tomas and his team, the bench's synthetic pool)

### Session 1 - What attention is, and what it is not
- Part 1: three jobs, not one span. Posner and Petersen's attention system: alerting (reaching and
  holding a ready state), orienting (selecting where to look or listen), executive control
  (resolving conflict between responses). [AS-abs Petersen and Posner 2012: the 1990 framework
  "elaborated and expanded"; AS-abs Fan et al. 2002: three networks, ANT in ~30 min, 40 adults,
  efficiencies "uncorrelated", with some interactions.] The 1990 article itself has no abstract
  online and was not read: cite it as the origin, quote nothing from it.
- Part 2: the goldfish myth traced (see facts). Attention is task-dependent, not a stopwatch.
- Part 3: what attention is not: not a single quantity, not a fixed span, not something that
  multitasks (the switching costs belong to time-management session 5, linked).
- Build-along: Tomas's attention diary, one working morning tagged by job (alert / orient /
  control) and by what pulled him off (constructed).

### Session 2 - When the mind leaves the task
- Part 1: Killingsworth and Gilbert 2010 (AS full text): what was sampled and found.
- Part 2: Smallwood and Schooler 2015 (AS-abs): perceptual decoupling, costs and benefits
  (errors vs creativity and future planning). Mind-wandering is not always bad.
- Part 3: catching it from outside: behavioural variability before mind-wandering reports
  (Seli, Cheyne and Smilek 2013, AS-abs); notifications can prompt task-irrelevant thought
  (Stothart et al. 2015, AS-abs). Meditation seam linked.
- Build-along: a self-probe log: a timer at random moments, three-option answer (on task /
  off task, aware / off task, unaware), in Tomas's day (constructed) and the learner's.

### Session 3 - Why focus fades over an hour
- Part 1: the vigilance decrement. Mackworth's clock test (1948, R: the classic origin, not read).
  Warm, Parasuraman and Matthews 2008 (AS-abs): vigilance is hard mental work and stressful, not
  understimulation; resource theory supported.
- Part 2: the disagreement: resource depletion vs mindlessness/mind-wandering vs goal habituation.
  Thomson, Besner and Smilek 2015 (AS-abs): neither theory alone explains the findings;
  resource-control account. Ariga and Lleras 2011 (AS-abs): brief goal switches averted the
  decrement, "a direct challenge" to depletion. Ego depletion (the self-control version of
  "a resource runs out"): Hagger et al. 2016 (AS-abs) 23 labs, N = 2,141, d = 0.04, 95% CI
  -0.07 to 0.15. Teach it: vigilance decrement is robust; its cause is contested; ego depletion
  failed a large replication; do not merge the two.
- Part 3: attention restoration theory. Kaplan (1995, R, origin not read). Berman, Jonides and
  Kaplan 2008 (AS-abs): nature walk or nature pictures improved backwards digit span and ANT
  performance. Ohly et al. 2016 (AS-abs): 31 studies, significant pooled effects for 3 measures
  (Digit Span Forward, Digit Span Backward, Trail Making B), the other 10 meta-analyses not;
  small samples and heterogeneity. Stevenson, Schilhab and Bentsen 2018 (AS-abs): 42 articles since
  July 2013, working memory and cognitive flexibility improved, attentional control "less
  reliable", low to moderate effects. Teach as: some support, uncertain which aspect of
  attention, exposure length confounds.
- Build-along: Tomas plans his afternoon around the drift (constructed).

### Session 5 - Designing the room and the phone
- Part 1: phone presence. Ward, Duke, Gneezy and Bos 2017 (AS-abs via RePEc summary: two
  experiments; phone presence reduced available cognitive capacity even when people did not check
  it; cost highest for the most smartphone-dependent). "Nearly 800" participants, phone in another
  room beat phone on desk: R (university news coverage), quote with "reported".
  Ruiz Pardo and Minda 2022 (AS-abs): pre-registered direct replication of Ward's experiment 2,
  six conditions (desk / pocket or bag / outside room x on / off), OSpan and go/no-go: "did not
  replicate". Hartmann et al. 2020 (AS-abs): no overall effect on short-term or prospective memory;
  a moderating effect for prospective memory in low-dependency participants. Teach the
  disagreement: the strong "mere presence drains your brain" claim did not replicate; moving the
  phone costs nothing, so it is still a cheap bet, not a proven fix.
- Part 2: notifications. Stothart, Mitchum and Yehnert 2015 (AS-abs): notifications alone
  disrupted an attention-demanding task, comparable to active phone use; proposed via mind
  wandering. Kushlev, Proulx and Dunn 2016 (AS full text): 221 UBC undergraduates, within-subjects,
  counterbalanced, one week alerts on and phone in reach vs one week Do Not Disturb and phone away.
  Inattention 2.38 vs 2.27 (SD .58 / .56), t(189) = 3.02, p = .003, d = .44; hyperactivity 2.11 vs
  2.01, d = .45. Self-reported symptoms in a non-clinical sample, not ADHD. Read the raw
  difference (0.11) next to the effect size.
- Part 3: the room: open plan, chat presence, signals of "do not interrupt". Practice-level
  advice, no sourced statistic; anything about open-plan offices is labelled practice, not finding.
- Build-along: Tomas's notification audit and room plan (constructed).

### Session 6 - A focus block you can keep
- Part 1: deep work as practice, not finding. Newport, Deep Work (2016, R: a book making an
  argument; quote no statistic from it).
- Part 2: breaks. Biwer et al. 2023 (AS-abs): 87 students, one day; self-regulated breaks
  (n = 35) vs 24 min study + 6 min break ("Pomodoro", n = 25) vs 12 + 3 (n = 27). Self-regulated
  breakers had longer sessions and breaks, more fatigue and distractedness, lower concentration and
  motivation; no difference in mental effort or task completion. Ariga and Lleras 2011 links back.
  The "52 minutes on, 17 off" figure: R (attributed to a 2014 DeskTime app-data blog post, not
  peer reviewed, not read). Teach: no study fixes one best interval.
- Part 3: keeping it: a block as a calendar commitment, a start ritual, a stop rule, review.
  The bench's own lesson: one short block measures little; a practice is judged over weeks.
- Build-along: Tomas designs a two-week focus-block experiment with a written stop rule
  (constructed).

## Verified facts (by claim)

### Attention networks
- Petersen and Posner 2012, Annu Rev Neurosci 35:73-89 (AS-abs): update of the 1990 article; the
  framework "elaborated and expanded"; new networks for orienting and executive functions; links to
  development, genetics, pathology.
- Fan, McCandliss, Sommer, Raz and Posner 2002, J Cogn Neurosci 14(3):340-347 (AS-abs): three
  networks, alerting, orienting, executive attention; ANT ~30 min; 40 adults; reliable single-subject
  estimates; efficiencies uncorrelated; some interactions (alerting and orienting modulate flanker
  interference).

### Goldfish myth
- Microsoft Canada Consumer Insights, "Attention spans" (2015): 12 seconds (2000) to 8 seconds
  (2013), goldfish 9 seconds, credited to "Statistic Brain" (R, report not opened; reported by WSJ
  2017 and BBC 2017, both read in a reproduction). Report's own research: survey of 2,000 Canadians
  and EEG of 112 people (R via TIME 2015, AS for TIME's wording).
- TIME, McSpadden, 14 May 2015, "You Now Have a Shorter Attention Span Than a Goldfish" (AS): states
  9 seconds for the goldfish with no source.
- WSJ, McGinty, 17 Feb 2017 (read in a course reproduction): the NLM and AP, listed as sources,
  could find no record; Statistic Brain did not answer; Vogel: "remarkably stable across decades";
  Posner: no real evidence it has changed since the late 1800s.
- BBC World Service, Maybin, 10 Mar 2017 (read in the same reproduction): Briggs: attention is
  "very much task-dependent"; fish researcher Huntingford: no evidence goldfish have short spans.
- Teach: there is no measured human "attention span" in seconds, and no goldfish figure either.

### Mind-wandering
- Killingsworth and Gilbert 2010, Science 330:932 (AS full text): iPhone experience sampling;
  database ~250,000 samples from ~5,000 people in 83 countries; analysed 2,250 adults (58.8% male,
  73.9% US, mean age 34). Mind wandering in 46.9% of samples and in at least 30% of samples during
  every activity except making love. Less happy when wandering (b = -8.79); pleasant topics 42.5% of
  wandering samples, unpleasant 26.5%, neutral 31%; no happier on pleasant topics than on task;
  activities explained 4.6% of within-person variance in happiness, mind wandering 10.8%.
  Time-lag analyses "strongly suggested" wandering was generally the cause. Caveats to teach: a
  self-selected sample of 2010 iPhone users, mostly US; correlational with time-lag inference.
- Smallwood and Schooler 2015, Annu Rev Psychol 66:487-518 (AS-abs): perceptual decoupling; content
  from episodic and affective processes; regulation relies on executive control; costs (errors) and
  benefits (creativity, future planning).
- Seli, Cheyne and Smilek 2013, JEP:HPP 39(1):1-5 (AS-abs): metronome response task; response
  variability in the 5 trials before mind-wandering reports greater than before on-task reports,
  replicated in 2 samples.

### SART and its measurement (bench)
- Robertson, Manly, Andrade, Baddeley and Yiend 1997, Neuropsychologia 35(6):747-758 (AS-abs, via
  MRC CBU and Plymouth pages): SART, withhold to rare targets (one in nine); 34 TBI patients and
  75 controls; errors correlate with self- and informant-reported everyday attention failures and
  with other sustained-attention tests; false presses preceded by shortening RTs.
- Seli, Jonker, Cheyne and Smilek 2013, Front Psychol 4:265 (AS full text): SART timing 250 ms digit
  + 900 ms encircled-x mask = 1150 ms; digits in runs of nine without replacement; five font sizes
  (120, 100, 94, 72, 48 pt); 630 trials, 70 no-go (11%); 108 analysed of 118 undergraduates. SART
  errors and RT correlated r = 0.77: the SART is susceptible to speed-accuracy trade-offs. Under
  go-slow instructions, 16% fewer errors attributable to the change in RT.
- PsyToolkit SART (AS for the implementation): digit 3 is no-go, 250 ms + 900 ms, 18 practice and
  225 trials; no published norm numbers.
- No external norm for commission or RT is printed on any page: none was read at source.

### Vigilance, depletion, breaks
- Warm, Parasuraman and Matthews 2008, Human Factors 50(3):433-441 (AS-abs).
- Thomson, Besner and Smilek 2015, Perspect Psychol Sci 10(1):82-96 (AS-abs).
- Ariga and Lleras 2011, Cognition 118(3):439-443 (AS-abs): goal habituation; sporadic recall of
  the memory digits averted the decrement.
- Hagger et al. 2016, Perspect Psychol Sci 11(4):546-573 (AS-abs): k = 23, N = 2,141,
  d = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.15].
- Mackworth 1948 clock test: R (origin of the decrement; not read; no number quoted).
- Biwer, Wiradhany, Oude Egbrink and de Bruin 2023, Br J Educ Psychol 93 Suppl 2:353-367 (AS-abs).

### Restoration

- Ohly et al. 2016, J Toxicol Environ Health B 19(7):305-343 (AS-abs).
- Stevenson, Schilhab and Bentsen 2018, J Toxicol Environ Health B 21(4):227-268 (AS-abs).
- Kaplan 1995, J Environ Psychol 15:169-182 (R, origin not read).

### Wellbeing claims checked and kept off the pages
"Attention span shorter than a goldfish" is the only one taught (traced, false). Not used: "sitting
is the new smoking", "10,000 steps", "the physiological sigh resets stress in one breath",
"8 glasses of water", "cortisol belly", "power poses". "Ego depletion" appears only as the failed
replication above.

Readings the pages may quote: drift grows with block length (4.3, 8.4, 10.9 points); banners raise
the last-fifth rate from 31.4% to 35.8% for the same 10 minutes; a break brings it to 28.6%; the
30-second task's built-in drift is 1.0 point while the measure reports 4.6 (noise), and two runs of
the same people agree at r = 0.11, against 0.41 for 3 minutes and 0.85 for 20.
Learner part: 3-minute blocks are 157 trials with 17 or 18 no-go; 30-second blocks are 26 trials
with 2 or 3 no-go. Pages never print a learner number.

## Not covered (honest list)

- Caffeine, nootropics, supplements and any dosing.
- Neuroimaging detail of the networks.
- Brain-training games and their transfer debate.
- Sleep (the hub has courses that own it).
